ECON 601 tutorial outline

Advanced Microeconomic Theory II (PhD core), Vancouver School of Economics, UBC

William Duan, teaching assistant

A baseline plan for the weekly tutorials, written from notes on the Winter 2026 sessions.1 Each year follows the same sequence, adjusted to the pace of that year’s lectures.

Purpose of the tutorials

Tutorials complement the lectures rather than repeat them. Each session works through a small number of problems, mostly from the problem sets and past exams.

Objectives

By the end of the term, students should have the following.

  1. Equilibrium as a fixed point. A proper understanding of Nash equilibrium and the related equilibrium concepts as fixed points, usually found by guessing a candidate and verifying it.
  2. Tools for finding equilibria. Familiarity with the standard techniques for finding them, such as ruling out candidates by profitable deviations, indifference conditions for mixed strategies, symmetry and stationarity, and threshold strategies.
  3. Command of the core material. Enough understanding of the course content to pass the PhD comprehensive exam in microeconomic theory, given suitable effort on review.
  4. A picture of the whole. A rough sense of how the theory builds from Nash equilibrium, through dynamic games and games of incomplete information, to mechanism design and the economics of information.

Schedule

The term has thirteen weekly sessions. A holiday occasionally costs one, and make-up or exam review sessions balance it out.

Week 1 · What a Nash equilibrium is. Equilibrium as stability, compared with Walrasian equilibrium. A guessing game used to contrast dominance, rationalizability and equilibrium.

Week 2 · Guess and verify. Ruling out candidates by profitable deviations, then confirming a guess. Applied to quantity competition, a volunteer’s dilemma in mixed strategies, and an all-pay contest.

Week 3 · Dynamic games. Strategies as complete plans of action, subgames, and subgame perfect equilibrium found directly rather than only by backward induction.

Week 4 · Incomplete information. Perfect Bayesian equilibrium and the restrictions placed on beliefs. A game with private types solved through each player’s interim problem.

Week 5 · Infinitely repeated games. A war of attrition and alternating-offer bargaining, solved by guessing a stationary, symmetric strategy. Coordination with noisy private signals.

Week 6 · Signalling. Solving for perfect Bayesian equilibrium by moving between strategies, beliefs and best responses. Cheap talk and how much information it can carry.

Week 7 · Mechanism design. Vickrey–Clarke–Groves mechanisms for allocating priority and dissolving a partnership. Symmetric bidding in a first-price auction.

Week 8 · Limits of mechanism design. Why budget balance, incentive compatibility and participation cannot always hold together. The revenue equivalence theorem.

Week 9 · Auctions two ways. The same auction solved by equilibrium bidding and by revenue equivalence. Nash, subgame perfect and perfect Bayesian equilibrium compared in one game.

Week 10 · Adverse selection. Market unravelling with hidden quality. Screening contracts in an insurance market.

Week 11 · Screening and signalling. The second-best menu of contracts and pooling. Job-market signalling and the intuitive criterion.

Week 12 · Moral hazard. First- and second-best contracts when effort is hidden, and why an incentive constraint must bind. A signalling problem where types differ in how well the signal works rather than in its cost.

Week 13 · Repeated games. When cooperation can be sustained, the one-shot deviation principle, and why tit-for-tat can be an equilibrium without being subgame perfect. A past comprehensive exam question on the value of information.

Year notes

Winter 2026. Thirteen sessions, followed by a week of extra in-person office hours before the final exam.
