ECON 600 tutorial outline

Advanced Microeconomic Theory I (PhD core), Vancouver School of Economics, UBC

William Duan, teaching assistant

A baseline plan for the weekly tutorials, refined over Fall 2025 and Fall 2026 and written from my notes on each session.1 Each year follows the same sequence, adjusted to the pace of that year’s lectures.

Purpose of the tutorials

Tutorials complement the lectures rather than repeat them. Each session works through a small number of problems, mostly from the problem sets and past exams, and uses them to make one or two ideas from lecture usable. Where the slides already cover a derivation well, the session supplies the interpretation instead.

Objectives

By the end of the term, students should have the following.

  1. Mathematical literacy. The ability to parse a theorem, including what its premise and conclusion are and what it does not say, and to write simple proofs of their own.
  2. Command of the core material. Enough understanding of the course content to pass the PhD comprehensive exam in microeconomic theory, given suitable effort on review.
  3. A picture of the whole. A rough sense of how microeconomic theory builds from individual choice up to general equilibrium.

Schedule

The term has twelve weekly sessions. A holiday occasionally costs one, and make-up or exam review sessions balance it out.

Week 1 · Choice and the standard of proof. Rational preferences, choice rules and the Weak Axiom of Revealed Preference, ending with a first complete proof at the level of rigour the course expects.

Week 2 · Continuity and utility representation. The continuity axiom, how to show a preference is not continuous, and the theorem that a rational, continuous preference has a continuous utility representation.

Week 3 · The consumer’s problem. Existence of a solution, Walras’ law, and the convexity and uniqueness of demand, with attention to which hypothesis does the work in each result.

Week 4 · Proving from structure. A methods session. Results about demand under quasilinear and homogeneous utility, and a comparison of two taxes, each proved directly from the structure of the problem rather than by computing demand.

Week 5 · Duality and welfare. How Marshallian demand, indirect utility, the expenditure function and Hicksian demand determine one another, why consumer surplus is not a reliable welfare measure, and Afriat’s theorem on rationalizing finitely many choices.

Week 6 · Testing rationality on data. Turning the generalized axiom of revealed preference into an algorithm and running it on choices from a laboratory experiment.

Week 7 · Choice over time. Present-biased preferences, commitment, and naive against sophisticated planners.

Week 8 · Choice under risk. The probability simplex, the continuity and independence axioms, and how to build counterexamples from a diagram.

Week 9 · Risk attitudes. The Allais paradox, risk aversion in portfolio choice, and precautionary saving.

Week 10 · Subjective expected utility. States, acts and subjective beliefs, and what choices between bets reveal about those beliefs.

Week 11 · Reference dependence and the firm. Personal equilibrium, the firm’s problem and returns to scale, and monotone comparative statics.

Week 12 · General equilibrium. Walrasian equilibrium in economies with production, and how linear technologies pin down prices.

Year notes

Fall 2025. Eleven sessions. Week 1 reviewed proof techniques and basic real analysis. The representation theorem in week 2 was proved in full. Testing rationality on data was introduced briefly within week 5 rather than given its own session, so the later weeks each moved forward by one.

Fall 2026. In progress. I ran the Math Camp for this cohort, so week 1 went straight to choice and revealed preference instead of reviewing analysis. The representation proof in week 2 was previewed rather than replicated. Testing rationality on data had a full session, and choice over time was combined with the start of choice under risk.
